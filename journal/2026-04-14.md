# Session 21 — First External Research: Writing-for-AI Citations

**Date:** 2026-04-14
**Focus:** Verify and add citations to writing-for-ai.md's evidence claims

## Context

This is the first session exercising Research Before Action as a primary activity. Twenty sessions of work have been entirely internal — pattern extraction, elevation, editorial cleanup, team reviews. The writing-for-ai.md citations task is the editorial roadmap's item 5 and the team-coach's R4.4 test case.

writing-for-ai.md made seven specific evidence claims with varying levels of citation. Some had partial citations, some had none. The task was honest verification — find out which are real, which are wrong, and fix accordingly.

**Researcher specialist:** Not activated. Fails the recurring-need test (first external research session). Dispatched to general-purpose sub-agent instead.

## Research Findings

All seven claims turned out to be grounded in real research. Zero hallucinated sources. But four needed corrections:

| Claim | Verdict | Action Taken |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| ETH Zurich AGENTbench — "2-3% decrease" | Partially verified | Corrected to 0.5–2%, added full citation (Gloaguen et al., arXiv:2602.11988) |
| Chroma Context Rot — shuffled fragments | Verified | Improved citation format (Hong, Troynikov & Huber, 2025) |
| Instruction density — "50-98%" | Partially verified | Corrected to 27–98%, "63%" to "63–69%", added citation (Jaroslawicz et al., arXiv:2507.11538) |

| ICLR 2025 — semantic similarity | Partially verified | Specified paper: Jin et al. (bare "ICLR 2025" was too vague) |
| Context window — 0.5% / 719% | Verified | Added citation (Ayyachamy et al., arXiv:2601.11564) |
| Anthropic guidance — self-check | Partially verified | Refined to "Claude Code best practices and context engineering guidance" |

Added a compact References section at bottom of file. Document grew from 55 to 64 lines — modest for full citations.

## Decisions

- **Number corrections prioritised over softening.** Original "2-3%" and "50-98%" were wrong enough to undermine credibility. In a document about precision for AI consumption, the citations need to be right.
- **Kept inline citations over footnotes.** The document is already structured as a list of claims with parenthetical sources. Inline citations are consistent with that style. The References section at bottom is for verifiability, not primary reading.
- **Did not expand editorial commentary.** The "pricing model" analogy in claim 5 and the editorial glosses throughout are original synthesis, not from the papers. Left as-is — they're clearly framed as interpretation, not as quotes.

## Research Before Action — First Test

This was the team-coach's R4.4 test case: does the team actually exercise Research Before Action when the work demands it?

**What worked:** General-purpose sub-agent handled the research competently. Found all six papers, verified specific numbers against source material, caught the discrepancies. The corrections are meaningful — three claims had numbers that didn't match their sources.

**What was surprising:** Nothing was hallucinated. The original writing-for-ai.md was generated from real research — the citations were just imprecise or incomplete, not fabricated. The original author knew the literature; the citations just didn't survive generation cleanly.

**Researcher specialist signal:** One session is not enough to trigger activation (need 3+). But the sub-agent spent ~7 minutes on web research across 34 tool calls. If more pattern files need citation verification or if new patterns require literature review, the economics shift. Watch for demand.

## Practice Observation

The self-referential nature continues: a document about writing precise documents for AI consumption had imprecise citations. The meta-irony is that the *content* was right (the principles work) but the *evidence trail* was sloppy — exactly the kind of "semantically similar but imprecise" problem the document itself warns about in claim 5. Fixing the citations practices what the document preaches.

The general-purpose sub-agent approach worked for a bounded research task (verify N specific claims). Whether it works for open-ended investigation (find all relevant literature on topic X) is untested.
